8x8 vs 10x10 block puzzle: which board size suits you?

Two extra rows look like a small change, but they change the whole rhythm of the game.

An 8x8 and a 10x10 block puzzle use the same rules, but they are not the same game. An 8×8 board has 64 squares and a 10×10 board has 100, so the bigger board gives you about 56 percent more space. The block shapes stay the same on both, which means that difference noticeably affects the difficulty, your room for error, your chances of building combos and how long a game tends to last. On this page we compare the two board sizes with real numbers, clear up the question of rotation and show which size each of our games uses.

8x8 vs 10x10: the key differences

  • Space: an 8×8 board has 64 squares, a 10×10 board has 100.
  • Difficulty: most players find 8×8 harder, because the same block covers a bigger share of the board.
  • Room for error: on 10×10 there is space to recover from a bad placement; on 8×8 one mistake can end the game within a few moves.
  • Completing lines: a row fills with 8 squares on 8×8 and 10 squares on 10×10, so lines close more often on the small board.
  • Combo potential: 10×10 leaves room to set up several lines at once, so long combos are easier to build.
  • Game length: in our experience 10×10 games tend to last longer, while 8×8 suits short, intense sessions. This is a tendency, not a measured figure, and it depends on how you play.

Why an 8x8 block puzzle is harder

In all three games we compare here, the biggest blocks are the 3×3 square (9 squares) and the straight five-bar. A 3×3 square covers about 14 percent of an 8×8 board but only 9 percent of a 10×10 board. On an empty board, the 3×3 square fits in 36 different positions on 8×8 and in 64 positions on 10×10. As the board fills up, the gap widens: on the small board you run out of space for the big square much earlier.

The five-bar tells the same story. A horizontal bar fits into a row in 4 positions on 8×8 and in 6 positions on 10×10. Since blocks cannot rotate, horizontal and vertical bars each need their own space, and on a small board it gets hard to keep room for both.

8×8 does have one advantage: a row needs only 8 squares. A five-bar and a three-bar finish a row on their own. Clears come often on the small board, but the board also fills just as fast. What makes 8×8 hard is that every move shows its consequences much sooner.

Building combos on a 10x10 block puzzle

With plenty of space on a 10×10 board, you can leave several rows one square short and close them one after another on the following moves. Those back-to-back clears grow your combo. In BlockNest each consecutive clear raises the multiplier on line points by one, up to ×5, and a move that clears nothing resets it. Clearing 4 or more lines in one move adds a +500 bonus, and emptying the whole board adds +300.

The trap of the big board is comfort. Spreading blocks everywhere because there is room fills the middle with scattered little gaps. If a big square arrives before your prepared rows close, you can lose the streak and the game together. Good play on 10×10 means closing your prepared lines while keeping at least one 3×3 area open in the middle.

Combos and streaks on 8x8

Long combos are harder on a small board, but not impossible. Block Route in BlockFour is played on 8×8, and its combo also multiplies line points up to ×5, with extra bonuses of +100, +250 and +500 for clearing 2, 3 or 4 lines. In Woodora Classic each extra line in the same move adds +50, and on consecutive clears the streak bonus grows by 25 each time. On 8×8, the way to protect a streak is to plan for at least one line every hand and never overfill the board chasing a huge combo.

Can you rotate blocks?

Rotation affects difficulty as much as board size does. In all three games compared here, blocks cannot be rotated: each orientation of a shape arrives as a separate block and goes in exactly as shown. That is why you need to save the right corner for an L block and keep separate space for horizontal and vertical bars. Without rotation, the pressure of a small board is felt even more. Falling Blocks in BlockFour is a different kind of game: pieces drop from the top of a 10×20 well, and there you can rotate them.

Which board size should you pick?

  • New to the genre, or looking for long, calm games? Start with 10×10. BlockNest uses this board and has no timer.
  • Want short, thoughtful games where every move matters? Choose 8×8. Woodora Classic has no timer and shows how many lines a block will clear before you drop it.
  • Want 8×8 against the clock? Try the 120 Seconds mode of Block Route in BlockFour, or Daily Route for a new 30-move challenge every day.
  • Comfortable on 10×10 already? Move to 8×8; the tidy habits you build on the small board will raise your score on the big one too.
Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is 8x8 or 10x10 harder?

For most players, 8×8. The blocks stay the same size, so each one takes up more of the board, you have less room for error and the board locks up sooner.

Do you score more on a 10x10 board?

Often, yes, because games tend to last longer and there is more room to build combos, though it is not a fixed rule. Scoring rules differ from game to game, though, so comparing high scores across different games is not meaningful.

Which board sizes do your games use?

BlockNest uses a 10×10 board and Woodora Classic an 8×8 board. Block Route in BlockFour is also 8×8, while Falling Blocks in the same app is played in a 10×20 well.

Can blocks be rotated?

Not in BlockNest, Woodora Classic or Block Route; every block goes in as shown. Only Falling Blocks in BlockFour lets you rotate pieces.

Can I change the board size inside a game?

No. Each game has a fixed board. To try the other size, switch games: BlockNest for 10×10, Woodora Classic or BlockFour for 8×8.
